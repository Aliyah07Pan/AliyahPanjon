Designing My Canvas: a dashboard that tells independent earners where they stand

TL;DR

Problem

The dashboard wasn't built around user needs, and partway through the project the target user changed.

My role

Solo UX / product designer: research, four personas, the dashboard redesign, and a mid-fidelity prototype of My Canvas.

Outcome

Reframed the product around one user and shipped the My Canvas prototype; now preparing a usability study.

Role

UX / Product Design

Team

Solo designer

Timeline

Jun 2026 – present

Tools

Figma, Claude, Claude Code

Platform

Website

Thearis is a financial planning platform. It began as an app for independent women, then pivoted mid-project to serve independent people who manage their own finances and run a side business. I ran the research, built the personas, redesigned the dashboard, and shipped a mid-fidelity prototype of My Canvas, the first screen people see after they log in, built to show them where they stand in seconds.

Overview

Thearis helps independent people make sense of money that comes in on an unpredictable schedule, personal and business side by side. I was brought on as a UX designer, but the work covered the full product design process: research, personas, the dashboard redesign, and a mid-fidelity prototype. Working solo meant I owned every decision, from the first research question to the final screen. My focus was My Canvas, the landing screen, which has to help someone understand where they stand financially within seconds without wasting their time.

Impact

  • Carried a mid-project pivot, from women-focused to independent earners with a side business, by keeping the research that still held and resetting the rest, including a move to a gender-neutral visual style.
  • Reorganized the product's information architecture around a sidebar and a single dashboard after persona walkthroughs surfaced seven usability issues in the existing design.
  • Designed the My Canvas mid-fidelity prototype, with separate personal and business sections, a combined status panel, and a pull-not-push AI assistant, grounded in Reddit research and behavioral principles.
  • Built four personas, a competitor SWOT, and a user flow; now preparing a usability study before building the design system in code.

The challenge

Two things were moving at once. A dashboard already existed, but it hadn't been built around what users actually needed, so it had to change. And partway through, the direction itself changed: in July the CFO and her financial advisors decided the target customer should be broader than independent women. The hard part wasn't adding features. It was deciding what research still applied, redesigning the first screen to feel calm to someone anxious about money, and serving a person whose financial situation had just changed and who needed to know whether they were okay.

Competitor analysis

To ground the redesign, I reviewed 10 products in women's financial planning and studied four in depth with a SWOT, scoring each on onboarding, navigation clarity, and task flow. Strengths and weaknesses describe each product; opportunities and threats are read against Thearis.

The design question

How might we make My Canvas show an independent earner where they stand, and what to do next, in the first few seconds after they log in?

Key decisions

The core of the work. Each one changed the product's direction.

01

One user, through a mid-project pivot

Research
I started women-focused: a competitor SWOT (10 products reviewed, 4 studied in depth: Ellevest, Fruitful, Willow, Range) and persona-based walkthroughs of the existing dashboard. In July the CFO and her advisors redirected the product to independent people who manage personal finances and run a side business.
Decision
I treated the earlier research as a starting point, not a sunk cost. I kept what held up, the demand for explained insights and visible goal progress, moved the visual style gender-neutral, and kept one tone rule from the start: no “don't worry” or “it's easier than you think” copy.
Why it mattered
Reassuring copy quietly patronizes. Stating facts and the next step plainly earns trust, and it works for any audience.
Result
Four personas carried the shift. Camilla and Danielle framed the original direction; Maya and Marcus, two independent earners with side businesses, anchored the new one.

Maya and Marcus are proto-personas, built from research themes and stakeholder input rather than interviews; names, jobs, and incomes are illustrative.

02

Rebuilding the structure before the screen

Research
The existing dashboard wasn't built around user needs. Persona-based walkthroughs surfaced seven issues, including inconsistent naming, competing navigation systems, and weak visual hierarchy.
Decision
I wrote a fix for each issue and reorganized the product into a single-page canvas: a global header, the canvas summary, and personal and business finances down the page, with deeper actions — review an opportunity, meet an advisor, see where you stand — opening in side panels and short flows instead of new screens.
Why it mattered
A clear spine lets the dashboard stay simple. Everything else gets a home off the main screen instead of competing for the first glance.
Result
A single-page architecture the prototype could be built on: the canvas stays scannable, and everything deeper lives one panel away instead of crowding the first glance.

Lo-fi wireframes

Before building, I blocked out the My Canvas layout as low-fidelity wireframes in Figma — settling the header, the sidebar areas, and how the canvas summary and finance sections stack before committing to visuals.

Prototype walkthrough

From the wireframes I built the My Canvas prototype: the personal and business views of the dashboard, Ask Thearis, and the side-panel flows for reviewing an opportunity, booking an advisor, and clearing a business task.

A system to build on

With the flows settled, I defined the visual language the design system will be built from in code: a gender-neutral palette and a Manrope type scale.

Outcome

I walked stakeholders through the prototype and made changes from their feedback. The product now has a user it's actually built for and a first screen that answers the question that user walks in with: am I okay, and what do I do next? I'm now preparing a usability study on the mid-fidelity prototype to see where people get stuck before I build the design system in code. I plan to measure whether My Canvas helps people take action, like setting a first goal, not just how long they spend on the page, since the barrier in finance is usually action, not information.

Reflection

Things move fast in a startup. The pivot didn't just change the screens, it changed who I was designing for and which research I could keep. Naming the user was the biggest lever: once I knew who My Canvas was for, every decision after it got easier.

Leaning on Claude and Claude Code moved me from research to working screens quickly, but the calls about what to build stayed mine. Next: run the usability study, refine based on what I find, and build the design system in code.

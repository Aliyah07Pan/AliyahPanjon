Chimera · Team project

What a user needs in the five seconds after a security alert

Chimera’s creator, Jay, had built his own security camera app, and could no longer make it make sense to anyone but himself. We audited the existing UI and ran usability testing with 6 participants across five core tasks; we rebuilt the flows around what those sessions surfaced: users needed visual confirmation of what the system had just done (logging in, refreshing, deleting), and the app’s own vocabulary (“scrubber,” “thumbnail”) was working against them, not for them.

TL;DR

Problem

Chimera’s creator built the app himself, and first-time users couldn’t make sense of it.

My role

UX design and research on a team of three: UI audit, usability testing with 6 participants, and the redesigned flows.

Outcome

6 of 6 participants deleted a video without help; confusing terms were renamed and a mobile registration flow was added.

Role

UX Design and Research

Team

Amy Ruiz, Hannah Repuyan, me

Timeline

3 weeks

Tools

Figma

Platform

Mobile app

Overview

Chimera is a security camera app that needed to work for first-time users, not just its creator. The redesign had to stay mobile-first and stay within Chimera’s frame-based system (not a full DVR-style video interface), and add a mobile registration flow that didn’t exist yet.

Impact

  • 100% (6/6) of usability participants completed the video-deletion task without assistance after the redesign.
  • Renamed “Scrubber” → “Footage” and “Generate Thumbnails” → “Generate Frames” after participants repeatedly stumbled on the app’s internal jargon (“I don’t know what a scrubber is. Once I got past that, it was easy.” P3).
  • Designed a mobile registration and role-based login flow that didn’t previously exist, closing a gap the app’s own creator had flagged.

The challenge

The founder had designed and built Chimera himself, and it showed: the interface made sense to him but not to first-time users. Any redesign had a hard constraint: it had to work within Chimera’s frame-based system, not become a full DVR-style video interface, and the mobile version needed a registration and login flow built from nothing.

The design question

How might we make Chimera intuitive for first-time users without abandoning the frame-based system it was already built on?

Key decisions

The core of the work. Each one follows the same path: Research → Insight → Decision → Outcome.

01

Alert to action

Research
There was no visual indicator of a successful login, and the homescreen had no clear hierarchy. An overloaded left nav and unbalanced layout left users unsure where to start.
Insight
In the moment a user actually opens this app, right after an alert, every extra second of confusion matters.
Decision
We moved navigation to the bottom of the screen for one-handed mobile use, simplified camera switching from the main nav, and restructured the homescreen into a dashboard.
Outcome
50% (3/6) of usability participants said they wanted a clearer visual confirmation after logging in, which was logged as a priority for the next iteration.

02

Live view vs. recorded footage

Research
On the live screen, there was no clear way to tell which camera was which, and no reliable way to refresh the feed. Separately, the screen for reviewing recorded footage relied on confusing internal terms and its video component disappeared entirely on mobile.
Insight
Users needed “what’s happening now” separated from “what happened before,” in language they actually understood instead of internal engineering terms.
Decision
We added distinct camera titles and visual blocking plus a labeled “Refresh feed” button on the live screen, and renamed “Scrubber” → “Footage” and “Generate Thumbnails” → “Generate Frames,” restoring the missing video component on mobile.
Outcome
“I don’t know what a scrubber is. Once I got past that, it was easy.” (P3). Direct evidence the renaming addressed a real comprehension barrier, not a cosmetic one.

03

Setting up a new camera

Research
There was no camera-registration screen at all. With no way to register a camera, there was no way for a user to view its footage.
Insight
It’s a first-time user’s very first interaction with the physical hardware, the point where confusion costs the most.
Decision
We designed a new registration screen with a required camera-name field, plus help buttons on the more technical configuration fields.
Outcome
An honest open question we flagged rather than smoothed over: the exact RTSP setup process wasn’t confirmed with the stakeholder, so several configuration fields are placeholders pending that detail.

04

False alarms & empty states

Research
Every usability participant (6/6) completed the task of deleting a large video file, but several wanted more certainty about exactly what they were about to permanently delete, since the footage stayed fully visible right up to the confirmation step.
Insight
An accidental, high-consequence deletion is most likely in exactly the kind of urgent moment this app is built for.
Decision
We recommended pairing the delete flow with a timeline slider showing what’s being removed, and blurring the footage on the confirmation screen to signal the deletion is final.
Outcome
Logged as a priority for the next iteration rather than shipped in this round (see Future Iterations in the deck).

Outcome

Testing the redesign across five tasks with 6 participants validated the direction (100% completed video deletion) while surfacing the next round of work: visual feedback, terminology, and icon clarity became this project’s key findings and fed directly into a future-iterations backlog.

Reflection

This project began with an existing product, shown here only in its redesigned form out of respect for confidentiality, and the full engagement was completed within a three-week timeline. Given more time, we would have run a second round of usability testing with more participants after iterating. The project taught us a great deal, especially about working within a tight deadline and collaborating closely as a team.

Archlog · Team project

Architecture students lose the “why” behind their design decisions

ArchLog is a design-decision tracker for architecture students: a place to capture why a move was made while it’s still happening, instead of reconstructing the story from memory the night before a review. We ran research with 17+ students, a focus group, and stakeholder-tested usability studies that showed the real gap wasn’t idea generation; it was traceability. We tested seven feature concepts head-to-head, and one won by a wide margin.

TL;DR

Problem

Architecture students lose the reasoning behind their design decisions by the time they reach critique.

My role

UX design and research on a team of three: a 17+ response survey, a focus group, A/B usability sessions, and the prototype.

Outcome

The Design Decision Tracker ranked first of seven concepts (average 1.75), with 3 of 4 participants naming it their top choice.

Role

UX Design and Research

Team

Aliyah Panjon, Amy Ruiz and Susan Chapas

Timeline

4 weeks

Stakeholder

Prof. Aurora Bonomi

Users

Architecture undergrad students

Overview

ArchLog is a decision-logging tool built for a workflow that has no standard pattern: architecture studio work. It pairs a project overview (visuals uploaded alongside each decision) with a decision log (what changed, why, and what it trades off), then turns that log into a critique-ready narrative so students walk into review prepared instead of reconstructing their thinking from scratch.

Impact

  • Tested seven feature concepts with focus-group participants; the Design Decision Tracker finished first by a wide margin, with 3 of 4 participants naming it their top choice.
  • Research (17+ survey responses, a 4-person focus group, 6 A/B usability sessions with students and stakeholder Prof. Aurora Bonomi) reframed the problem itself: the gap isn’t ideation, it’s traceability, a finding that shaped which of seven concepts we built.
  • Confirmed the design direction against a real stakeholder before committing: two directions were pressure-tested in A/B usability studies before ArchLog’s current structure was chosen.

The challenge

Across studio environments, students struggle to structure their design process, and once they’re moving, there’s no consistent way to capture why they made the moves they made. Critiques rely on memory, while the actual decisions live scattered across notebooks, Miro boards, and “shower thoughts,” creating a fragmented, hard-to-defend narrative that varies by project and by critic.

The design question

How might we create an interactive, low-friction tool that enables architecture students to develop concepts with greater clarity within the studio environment?

Research

We surveyed architecture students about how they find inspiration, where the process gets hard, and what would keep them using a tool.

Where inspiration comes from

Architecture precedents (94%), social platforms like Pinterest and Instagram (82%), and sketching (71%) were the most common sources across 17 responses.

The hardest part

Iteration (45%) and developing concepts (36%) topped the list, followed by explaining the design (18%).

What keeps students coming back

Over half (55%) said skill improvement would motivate consistent use, ahead of studio performance and gamification (18% each).

Choosing a concept

Four participants ranked seven concepts. The Design Decision Tracker ranked first with an average of 1.75, well ahead of Precedent Deconstruction (4.0).

05

High-fidelity screens

Each project is organized into four tabs: Project Overview, Log Decision, Decision History, and Narrative Builder. The overview holds the brief, constraints, milestones, notes, and reflection prompts. Log Decision captures a decision in plain language and surfaces related past ones. Decision History lists every entry with tags and filters, and the Narrative Builder turns selected decisions into a critique-ready narrative. A profile page collects each student's projects and lets them share public ones.

Key decisions

The core of the work. Each one follows the same path: Research → Insight → Decision → Outcome.

01

What counts as a decision worth logging

Research
Decision-making lived entirely in students’ heads. Ideas surfaced in fragments (sketches, conversations, “shower thoughts”) and rarely survived to critique in a form anyone could point back to.
Insight
Students needed external scaffolding for their reasoning, so a decision has a record the moment it’s made, not weeks later.
Decision
We pushed to make decision logging the core primitive of the tool: capture what changed, why, and what it improves or trades off, rather than a generic notes field.
Outcome
The Design Decision Tracker concept topped focus-group rankings: 3 of 4 participants picked it as their top choice out of seven concepts tested.

02

Low-effort capture during studio work

Research
2 in 3 surveyed students said their approach “depends on the project.” There’s no shared method, and studio schedules leave no room for a tool that adds friction.
Insight
The log had to be light enough to use mid-project, not just as a pre-deadline scramble.
Decision
We paired decision logging with a project overview so students upload visuals for each decision, keeping the iteration and its rationale in the same place rather than a separate tool.
Outcome
This visual-plus-rationale pairing was one of the two directions pressure-tested in A/B usability studies with students and Prof. Bonomi before being carried forward.

03

Retrieval and review for critiques

Research
7 of 17 surveyed students named concept development as the hardest phase, and critique is exactly where the gap showed up: without a record of why, students reconstruct their narrative from memory hours before review.
Insight
The burden of proof needed to move from memory to a persistent record, so students walk into reviews prepared, not panicked.
Decision
We designed the design-narratives feature to generate a critique-ready story directly from the decision log.

04

Structure that fits how reviews run

Research
Constraints were repeatedly described by students as “creative fuel,” not a limitation, but studio culture rewards adaptability without giving anyone a structure to lean on.
Insight
The tool needed a full loop: capture, narrate, act on feedback, carry the record forward.
Decision
We rounded the tool out with task management (tracking feedback and action items from crits) and designer profiles (exporting projects so crits can follow the thinking), extending structure past the log itself.

Outcome

Of seven concepts tested, the Design Decision Tracker won by a wide margin, confirming the research insight that architecture students’ gap isn’t ideation, it’s traceability.

Reflection

This project taught me that the most useful research finding can reframe the problem itself. We set out to help students generate ideas, but the data pointed to traceability instead. With a 4-week timeline and a small participant pool, we tested concepts more than the finished prototype. It reinforced the value of truly listening to users and refining what will benefit them in the long term.
